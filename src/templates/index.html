About the five atomic models (static, crawlable)

Dalton — Solid Sphere (1803)

Each element is made of tiny, indivisible, identical spheres. Mass distinguishes elements; they combine in fixed whole-number ratios.

Limitations

Atoms contain protons, neutrons and electrons, and occur as isotopes; the solid sphere cannot explain spectra, bonding, or radioactivity.

Thomson — Plum Pudding (1904)

A positively charged sphere with electrons embedded throughout, like plums in a pudding, neutral overall.

Limitations

Cannot explain large-angle alpha scattering or discrete line spectra.

Rutherford — Nuclear (1911)

Atoms are mostly empty space with a tiny, dense, positive nucleus; electrons orbit at a distance.

Limitations

A classical orbiting electron should radiate and collapse; the model cannot explain discrete spectra.

Bohr — Quantised Orbits (1913)

Electrons occupy fixed circular orbits with quantised energies, grouped into shells (n = 1, 2, 3…). Exact for hydrogen.

Limitations

Only accurate for one-electron atoms; many-electron atoms need quantum mechanics, not fixed circles.

Quantum — Probability Clouds (1926)

Electrons are described by wave functions whose square gives a probability density — orbitals (s, p, d, f), not fixed paths.

Limitations

Exact solutions exist only for hydrogen-like atoms; many-electron atoms require approximation methods.
